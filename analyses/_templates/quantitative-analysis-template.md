# Quantitative analysis title

## Executive summary

State the numerical claim, the principal methodological problem or strength, the evidence score and the real-world significance.

## 1. Exact claim

Quote or accurately reproduce the claim without expanding its meaning.

## 2. Claim classification

Select one or more:

- descriptive;
- comparative;
- predictive;
- causal;
- value-laden claim expressed through a number.

## 3. What should be measured?

Define the real phenomenon, legal duty, public objective or outcome that the claim is supposed to represent.

## 4. What was actually measured?

Identify the indicator, proxy, administrative output or model result used in place of the underlying phenomenon.

## 7. Baseline and comparison

Identify the comparison year, group, institution, target or counterfactual. Explain whether the compared units are genuinely comparable.

## 8. Distribution and variation

Where relevant, examine:

- mean and median;
- range and extreme cases;
- cases outside the legal deadline;
- subgroup differences;
- missing values;
- changes in classification;
- survivorship or selection effects.

## 10. Correlation and causality

Assess:

- common causes;
- reverse causality;
- simultaneous policy or external changes;
- regression to the mean;
- whether the design supports causal inference;
- whether a plausible comparison or counterfactual exists.

Do not describe temporal sequence or correlation as causation without adequate evidence.

## 12. Quantitative formalism indicators

Check for:

- activity formalism;
- closed-case formalism;
- percentage without denominator;
- average hiding extreme cases;
- deadline average hiding overdue cases;
- budget execution presented as achieved impact;
- inspection or sanction counts presented as cessation of harm;
- favourable baseline selection;
- non-representative sample;
- correlation presented as causation;
- graphical or ranking distortion;
- false precision;
- AI output presented as neutral fact without inputs, criteria and human review.

## 13. Alternative explanations

List credible explanations that could produce the observed result without the claimed cause.

## 14. Material and legal significance

Translate the number into practical effect: rights realised, harm reduced, decisions implemented, money saved or spent, people affected, delay avoided, inequality changed or public mission achieved.

## 15. Missing evidence

Identify the exact table, raw record, metadata, methodology, audit trail or comparison needed to confirm or reject the claim.

## 16. Evidential-strength score

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 0 | Claim without verifiable data. |
| 1 | Anecdotal, fragmentary or unverifiable data. |
| 2 | Descriptive data without an appropriate comparison. |
| 3 | Persuasive association without sufficient basis for causality. |
| 4 | Strong comparative or quasi-experimental evidence with clear assumptions. |
| 5 | Strong causal evidence, reproducible method, clear limitations and confirmation from multiple sources. |
