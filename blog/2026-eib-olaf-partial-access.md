## Executive summary

A journalist asked the European Investment Bank (EIB) for documents concerning investigations of EIB staff by the European Anti-Fraud Office (OLAF) between 2015 and 2023. The request covered OLAF final reports and recommendations as well as the EIB’s final disciplinary decisions.

The EIB initially refused access to all the documents. Following a request for review, it granted partial access to ten of thirteen OLAF reports but continued to withhold three reports and the disciplinary follow-up documents. It relied on the protection of investigations, personal data and its decision-making process.

The European Ombudsman inspected the documents and found no maladministration in the refusal to provide full access. A significant amount of the information could make individual staff members identifiable, and some OLAF follow-up proceedings were still ongoing.

But the decision contains an important qualification: not all information in the withheld disciplinary decisions was personal data. Some information was purely administrative. The Ombudsman therefore suggested that the EIB reassess the decisions and consider disclosing those portions that can be released without creating a risk of identification.

That distinction is the core accountability lesson of the case.

## The institutional sequence

The public record establishes the following sequence:

1. In June 2024, a journalist requested documents relating to OLAF investigations into EIB staff for the period 2015–2023.
2. In August 2024, the EIB refused access to all requested documents.
3. The requester submitted a confirmatory application asking the EIB to review that refusal.
4. In February 2025, the EIB granted partial access to ten OLAF reports but continued to withhold three OLAF reports and its disciplinary follow-up decisions.
5. The EIB provided a one-page overview describing how it had followed up OLAF’s disciplinary recommendations.
6. The requester complained to the European Ombudsman in March 2025.
7. The Ombudsman opened an inquiry and inspected the documents.
8. On 29 July 2026, the Ombudsman found no maladministration in the refusal of full access but suggested a new assessment of purely administrative information contained in the disciplinary decisions.

The sequence matters because the Ombudsman did not simply choose between full disclosure and full confidentiality. The inquiry tested what information existed inside the protected documents and whether all of it required the same treatment.

## What the EIB disclosed about the follow-up

According to the Ombudsman’s decision, OLAF issued 31 disciplinary recommendations to the EIB. The EIB said it took follow-up action in all 31 cases.

At the time described in the decision, 28 cases were closed, two were suspended while proceedings continued at national level and one remained ongoing at the EIB.

Among the 28 closed cases, three went before a Disciplinary Committee: one resulted in dismissal, one in a reduced pension for eight years and one ended without a sanction. Sixteen cases were handled through disciplinary proceedings without a Disciplinary Committee and ended with written reprimands. Six ended with written warnings and three without further measures.

The EIB also stated that all relevant recoveries following OLAF financial recommendations had been completed.

This aggregate disclosure is important. It demonstrates that institutional accountability can provide meaningful information about follow-up without necessarily identifying the individuals concerned.

## Core forensic question

> When a document contains protected personal data, has the institution demonstrated that the non-personal parts cannot be disclosed separately?

This is a more precise question than asking whether a document “contains personal data”.

A document can contain personal data and administrative information at the same time. The presence of protected information does not logically establish that every sentence, heading, procedural field or administrative element must remain confidential.

The relevant accountability exercise is therefore granular: identify the protected information, assess the risk of direct or indirect identification, examine whether redaction can control that risk, and determine whether the remaining information can be released.

## What the Ombudsman accepted

The Ombudsman accepted important parts of the EIB’s reasoning.

For the three withheld OLAF reports, follow-up activities were still ongoing. Two were connected to judicial proceedings at national level and one to administrative follow-up within the EIB. The Ombudsman considered the reliance on the general presumption of confidentiality for those reports justified.

The inspection also confirmed that the disciplinary material contained a significant amount of personal data. Even apparently neutral information can contribute to identification when combined with other information available from different sources. The risk therefore cannot be assessed by looking at isolated words or fields alone.

Given this context and the summary already supplied by the EIB, the Ombudsman considered the refusal of full public access overall reasonable and closed the case without a finding of maladministration.

## The important qualification

The inspection nevertheless showed that not everything in the withheld material constituted personal data.

The Ombudsman identified a limited amount of information that was administrative in nature. This produced a suggestion for improvement: the EIB should reassess its disciplinary follow-up decisions with a view to granting access to portions containing purely administrative information where disclosure would not risk revealing personal data.

This is a narrow finding, but an important one.

It rejects a binary model in which a file is treated as either public or confidential in its entirety. Instead, it points toward a document-level and passage-level assessment.

## Formalism risk

The case illustrates a recurring form of administrative formalism:

**Protected-data formalism:** “The document contains personal data; therefore the document cannot be disclosed.”

That statement may identify a legitimate legal interest, but it does not complete the analysis. A defensible refusal should also show why redaction, extraction of administrative information or partial access would not adequately protect that interest.

The opposite error must also be avoided. Removing names does not automatically anonymise a disciplinary record. Job function, dates, circumstances, sanctions and information available elsewhere may allow an individual to be identified indirectly.

The correct test is therefore neither automatic secrecy nor automatic redaction. It is evidence-based assessment of identification risk.

## Expected evidence trace

A robust administrative file dealing with a similar access request should allow an external reviewer to reconstruct:

- which documents were identified as falling within the request;
- which passages were classified as personal data;
- which passages contained purely administrative information;
- how direct and indirect identification risks were assessed;
- what external information was considered relevant to possible re-identification;
- whether partial access and redaction were tested;
- why particular redactions were considered insufficient, where applicable;
- which investigations or follow-up proceedings remained ongoing;
- what aggregate information could be released safely;
- who made the final disclosure decision and on what evidential basis.

Without that trace, a reference to “personal data” risks functioning as a conclusion rather than an explanation.

## What the case does — and does not — demonstrate

The decision does not establish that the EIB unlawfully concealed disciplinary files. The Ombudsman expressly found no maladministration in the refusal to provide full access.

Nor does it establish that all administrative fragments must necessarily be disclosed. Information that appears non-personal in isolation can still contribute to identification in context.

What the case does establish is narrower and more useful: a legitimate confidentiality interest does not eliminate the need to examine the internal structure of a document.

The existence of protected information and the scope of permissible secrecy are two different questions.

## Civic Forensics test

For citizens, journalists and watchdog organisations, the case suggests a practical sequence when an institution refuses access because documents contain personal data:

1. Ask whether the institution assessed the document as a whole or individual passages.
2. Ask which categories of information create an identification risk.
3. Ask whether redaction or partial access was considered.
4. Ask whether purely administrative information can be separated from personal information.
5. Ask whether aggregate follow-up data can be disclosed.
6. Compare the claimed confidentiality with information the institution has already made public.

The purpose is not to force disclosure of protected personal information. It is to test whether confidentiality has been applied only as far as necessary.

## Preliminary finding

The strongest lesson from the EIB–OLAF case is that **personal data is a protection that requires analysis, not a label that automatically closes an entire file**.

The Ombudsman accepted that substantial confidentiality was justified while simultaneously identifying information that deserved another disclosure assessment. Those conclusions are not contradictory. Together, they illustrate what proportionate transparency should look like.

For evidence-based public accountability, the decisive question is therefore not simply whether an exception exists. It is whether the institution can demonstrate, at the level of the actual information requested, how far that exception needs to reach.

## Limitations

This analysis is based on the European Ombudsman’s published decision. Civic Forensics has not independently inspected the withheld OLAF reports or EIB disciplinary decisions. The analysis therefore addresses the transparency reasoning and evidence structure described in the public decision, not the merits of the underlying individual disciplinary cases.
